# Deep research

1. **Write the question down.** One sentence, plus what the answer will be used for. Split it into 3 to 6 sub-questions. Note any date or region that matters.
2. **Search several angles** with web_search. For each sub-question try at least two phrasings: the plain one, and one with a specific term ("2025 annual report", "site:gov", "pricing page", "changelog").
3. **Open the sources.** Use fetch_page on the best results. Search snippets are not evidence. Prefer, in order: official pages and filings, original data, reputable reporting, then blogs and forums.
4. **Take notes as you go.** For every fact keep: the claim, the number, the date it was published, the URL. Write these into a scratch file (`notes.md`) so they survive a long session.
5. **Cross-check.** Any number, date or name that matters needs two independent sources, or one primary source. If two sources disagree, record both and say why they might differ (different year, different definition, outdated page).
6. **Check freshness.** Look at the publish or update date. Prices, versions, laws and statistics go stale. Say "as of <date>" in the brief.
7. **Stop when** every sub-question has an answer you can point to, or you have clearly hit a gap. Say what you could not find.

**The brief** (keep it under about one page):
- Answer first, in two or three sentences.
- Key findings as short bullets, each with its source URL in brackets.
- Conflicts or uncertainty: what is disputed and by whom.
- Gaps: what is unknown and how to find out.
- Sources list with the date you read each page.

Rules:
- Never state a figure you did not see on a page. If you are estimating, label it as an estimate.
- Do not cite a URL you did not open.
- Quote sparingly and exactly; paraphrase the rest.
- Separate what the source says from what you conclude.
- If the question touches health, law or money decisions, say plainly that the user should confirm with a professional.
